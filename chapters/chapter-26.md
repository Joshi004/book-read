# Chapter 26 — The Elements of Behavior

> *"Crossing your arms is defensive. Well, there is a hint of truth in this translation — but the actual meaning reveals much more."*

This chapter contains a full description of every cell in the Behavioral Table of Elements. The BTE was built for limitless use — there's no need to take notes when you're using it for people-watching, or for reading someone's body language in a video you're studying. The descriptions that follow will help you understand the meaning of the behavior within each cell of the table, and the variations therein.

---

## How to Read This Chapter

Chapter 25 walked through the anatomy of a single BTE cell — the fourteen data points packed into that small square, from the Symbol in the corner to the Deception Timeframe letter tucked in the bottom right. This chapter puts that anatomy to work, cell by cell, in the table's own reading order.

Each entry below follows the same shape:

> **### Corrected Full Name — SYMBOL**
> *Gesture Type · Body Region · Deception Rating · Timeframe*
>
> The cleaned-up explanation of what the behavior means, why it happens, and what to look for — including every variation, caveat, and research citation Charles gives for it.

The italic line under each heading is a compressed version of that cell's data straight from the table — read it the same way you'd read a spec sheet. If a field is blank, it means that field doesn't apply to that particular behavior (a facial expression has no "gesture type" in the Open/Closed sense, for instance, and a handful of cells — like Blink Rate or Breathing Rate — are rated on their own variable scale rather than a fixed DRS number).

### The Table at a Glance

Before diving into individual cells, it helps to know what you're looking at when you open the full table (Figure 26.1, below). The table uses color and letter-coding to pack a huge amount of information into a small space:

::: definition
**Reading the Colors**
- **Green background** — the least stressful, most open behaviors on the chart.
- **Tan background** — behaviors indicating slight discomfort or stress.
- **Yellow background** — higher-discomfort behaviors.
- **Grey background** — the highest-stress behaviors on the table, rated 4.0.
- **Turquoise background** — facial expressions and microexpressions.
- **Blue background** — variable cells, which can present at different values (breathing rate running fast or slow, for instance) rather than a single fixed score.
- **Red letters** — if a behavior with red lettering is observed in the same window as another 4.0-rated behavior, it automatically becomes a 4.0 itself. Watch for these in combination with other high-stress cells.
- **Blue letters** — temperature increases the frequency of this behavior in everyone. In cold environments, these behaviors can be discounted or lowered in point value.
:::

And the two axes, as Chapter 25 covered: **vertically**, cells run from head-region behaviors down through the feet, then below the body entirely into Object Interaction and Direct Verbal behavior. **Horizontally**, cells run from low stress and low deception likelihood on the left, toward the highest-stress, most deception-associated behaviors on the right.

![Figure 26.1 — The complete Behavioral Table of Elements, high-resolution reference. Every cell referenced throughout this chapter appears here in its original position, with its full fourteen-point data set.](../assets/diagrams/chapter26_bte_full_table.png)

::: callout
**Deception scoring, as a reminder.** Deception is rated per question-and-answer scenario. If a subject's behaviors tally more than 11 points on the Deception Rating Scale during a single question-and-answer window, deception is highly likely — the Chapter 25 walkthrough of Mr. Phillips is the worked example to revisit if you need a refresher on how the math comes together.

## Arm Cross, Head, Face & Neck

### Arm Cross — ACC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: A*

The arm cross is used universally and carries four variations that matter greatly to the interviewer. This gesture shows up constantly in magazines, detective shows, and books, and all of these sources tend to say the same thing: crossing your arms is defensive. There is a hint of truth in that reading, but the actual meaning reveals much more.

When observing this behavior, note the closeness of the palms to the body, the direction the thumbs are pointing, the tightness of the cross, the flexion of the fingers, and the distance from the humerus, a paran,<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "a paran" immediately after "humerus" — possibly a parenthetical gloss for the term (e.g., "the upper arm bone"); exact original wording uncertain --> to the torso. These variations all broadcast small but significant details about the gesture's true meaning. Crossing the arms is something we do when we need reassurance, or when we feel threatened.

Here are a few generalized rules to follow when observing arm-crossing behavior. The tighter the grip, the stronger the need for reassurance. When we need reassurance, the arms pull in toward the body, almost as though we're hugging ourselves. Thumbs protruding upward is an almost certain sign of confidence, despite the crossed arms. Digital flexion increases in sync with the level of anger being experienced. Arm crossing, when grouped with posture and facial expression, can be a clear sign of pride. Hands wrapped around the upper arms, together with an associated display of AG (Anger) or CO (Contempt), is an intimidation attempt. When women do this during socially stressful situations, it's almost always accompanied by SH. When the fingers wrap around the arms and pull toward the sternum, look for AG — this is a warning sign. If you see this alongside WD, your subject is about to become violent. The dominant hand is almost always the one on top, or — put another way — the non-dominant arm is the one on the bottom.

Four variable factors should be annotated in the notebook for every arm-cross observation:

Variations:
- ACC1 — arm cross with palms touching the body
- ACC2 — arm cross with hands wrapped around the arms
- ACC3 — arm cross with palms touching the body and thumbs pointing upward
- ACC4 — arm cross with clenched fists, with or without amplifying digital flexion

### Head Tilt — HT
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Head · Deception Rating: DNL*

Head tilt is an open and vulnerable gesture. We tilt our heads when we are curious, flirtatious, or attempting to appear innocent — this is why this gesture is listed alongside SQ, YE, and IP as confirming behaviors. You'll see PE (Palm Exposure) appear when someone is explaining a point they haven't communicated very clearly, in a friendly environment.

The first conflicting behavior listed is JC. This behavior could indicate that the gesture was actually a sign of anger, aggression, or a challenge — males, and sometimes females, will expose vital parts of the body in order to appear fearless to an adversary. The second conflicting behavior is TP, since head tilt is sometimes associated with boredom. Feigned boredom is far more likely to present with a head tilt than actual boredom is.

Some research, including that of Desmond Morris, details the possible or observed differences between the leftward and rightward tilt of the head. Thus far, no significant study has been conducted to validate these observations, and the direction of tilt is irrelevant in the BTE.

### Gestures Absent — GA
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Body · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: DA*

We often expect a certain level of congruence between someone's words, syntax, sentiment, and tone, and their nonverbal behavior, including gestures. This congruence helps us understand and believe what a person is saying. For instance, if someone is telling a happy story, we expect to see corresponding happy gestures — open body language, animated hand movements, a smiling face.

However, when there's an absence of gestures that match the syntax, sentiment, or tone — what we call gestural absence — it can create a sense of incongruence or dissonance. This might make us question the authenticity of what's being said, or feel that something is off. For example, if someone is speaking in an excited tone about a recent achievement, but their body language is closed off and their gestures are minimal or nonexistent, this gestural absence could suggest that they don't feel as positive as their words suggest. Perhaps they're downplaying their achievement, or they aren't comfortable expressing excitement.

> **Definition.** Gestural absence is what you're seeing when someone who is normally expressive with their nonverbal behavior suddenly stops gesturing altogether.

### Chin Thrust — CT
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Head · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Chin thrust is a gesture with cultural implications in the Middle East and in some countries such as southern Ukraine and the Slovak Republic. In those regions, the chin thrust is used very regularly in conversation to indicate agreement, and to point in a direction — in place of the extended finger and arm used in America.

In the BTE, there are two variations: one used as a deliberate movement during conversation, and the other a seemingly involuntary, short-lived micro-gesture. In the first variation, the majority use the chin thrust to communicate anger, dissatisfaction, or challenge between competing individuals; in the USA, chin thrust is usually a challenge, due to the deliberate exposure of vital organs to another person. The second variation is actually a ventilating gesture used mostly by men — a way to adjust the collar of a collared shirt without having to use their hands to do so.

You'll notice that the sexual propensity field contains 1U/2M, meaning variation one is universal, and variation two is performed mostly by men.

Variations:
- CT1 — a deliberate conversational movement, used to indicate agreement or point direction (Middle East, southern Ukraine, Slovak Republic); in the USA, generally read as a challenge. Universal.
- CT2 — an involuntary, short-lived micro-gesture; a ventilating movement letting men adjust a collared shirt without using their hands. Mostly male.

### Eyebrow Flash — EF
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The eyebrow flash is common across almost every developed culture. In performing this gesture, subjects will quickly raise and lower their eyebrows, usually upon greeting another person. Anger, by contrast, makes the face crumple and lowers the eyebrows significantly — the eyebrow flash raises the eyebrows instead, and is used to convey friendliness, trustworthiness, and vulnerability.

Instantly raised eyebrows can also indicate anger and fear, and are used frequently to communicate an extremely important point with emphasis — parents will often do this when scolding children.

Variations:
- EF1 — the eyebrow flash
- EF2 — the constantly raised eyebrows expression

> **Note.** When people see someone give them an eyebrow flash, an unconscious phenomenon takes place that causes most people to return the eyebrow flash gesture unconsciously — and most aren't even aware they've made the expression after they've done it.

### Head Downcast — HD
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Head · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The downcast head has multiple meanings depending on contextual reference, but the majority of these gestures are used when a subject is experiencing shame, guilt, submissiveness, or personal anger. The tilde symbol before the U in the sexual propensity field indicates that both sexes perform this action, though it is sometimes used more often by women, especially during the early stages of flirtation and courtship.

GPR is listed here as a conflicting behavior, since its presence indicates the downward head behavior is displaying a meaning contrary to its typical one. This is seen regularly in abused spouses, sexually abused children, and children who are frequently bullied in school.

### Lip Compression — LC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: B*

Several gestures involve lip compression, but this expression is almost universally indicative of someone suppressing their opinions. This action creates a barrier that prevents them from speaking, and you'll see it in conversations on a daily basis. Lip compression is usually performed when someone is holding back information — it does not mean deception is likely at all. Social rules and norms, peer pressure, and societal influences cause us to hold our tongues regularly in conversation.

The only time it becomes extremely significant is when you're observing a subject listening to a hypothetical reenactment of a crime, or being given possible reasons that the crime committed is an understandable and somewhat acceptable offense given the circumstances. This should sound alarms, and should increase the LC deception-likelihood number to a 3.0. This scenario is simply an example, and should not stop an analyst from making a judgment call on a behavior involving LC that seems deceptive in context.

### Teeth Sucking — TS
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: B*

This behavior is almost universally antisocial in conversation, and becomes an aggressive behavior when deliberately performed in the conversational presence of others. It's usually performed as a challenge to another person, to express latent disrespect or contempt. There's even a Jamaican text abbreviation for this: KMT, standing for "kiss my teeth," which describes the behavior very well.

Variations:
- TS1 — sucking teeth as a deliberate antisocial act
- TS2 — sucking teeth as a hygienic gesture

### Turtling — TU
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Face/Body · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: BDA*

This behavior is seen when the shoulders draw upward and the head lowers downward simultaneously. It protects the neck and other vital organs, and is a fear-based gesture. Children who have been abused will frequently display this in interviews and when approached by unknown strangers (Darwin, 1872). These children will also reflexively turtle their bodies in response to sudden movements by nearby abusive parents (Shuts, 1958).<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "Shuts, 1958" — possibly "Schutz, 1958"; exact original citation uncertain -->

The gesture also occurs in adults when fear is present, and should be contextually relevant before it raises any suspicion of deception in interview scenarios. This is a temperature-linked behavior, and is more common in colder temperatures.

### Object in Mouth — OM
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: B*

The insertion of any object into the mouth by a subject is not likely to be a deceptive behavior. This behavior is performed mostly out of a need for reassurance, or due to uncertainty — men and women do this equally. A good rule of thumb is to assume that any time an object or finger passes the barrier of the lips, that person needs reassurance.

This cell also includes lip biting, when the lip itself passes the teeth and goes into the mouth. A simple lip bite can be annotated in the comments section of the accompanying gesture block in the interrogator's notebook.

### Jaw Clench — JC
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: Before/After*

This nonverbal cue is seen in the jaw muscles and the temple area. It suggests that the subject is withholding aggressive action or feelings, and can also signify latent hostility or anger at what is being done or discussed.

### Nostril Wing Dilation — WD
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 3.0 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

All mammals require oxygen to survive. As our hearts beat faster in response to stress, the body sends a signal that more oxygen is required to meet the needs of the impending adrenaline. In conversation and social settings, instead of taking a giant breath, you will see the slight flaring of the nostrils when this occurs — a way for the body to increase blood oxygenation by taking in a larger volume of air.

Situations that create physical or emotional arousal produce some of the same neurochemicals as stress. Likewise, you will observe wing dilation — the nonverbal term for this behavior — in emotionally aroused subjects engaged in conversation. You'll see more wing dilation in attraction and arousal scenarios, while the subject is listening to someone speak that they are attracted to or aroused by.

### Confirmation Glance — CG
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

During the course of an interview or conversation, subjects will tend to look at a person or a group of people to confirm that a statement has been understood, and sometimes to confirm they are being believed. Following a statement, a cursory glance at another party in the room to verify that their story is working on the group is called a confirmation glance. This behavior can occur before, during, or after a statement, but it's more likely to occur just after a statement, answer, or denial of guilt in an interview setting. In social settings, you'll see that confirmation glances always tend toward the most socially influential or powerful person in the group while people are engaged in conversation.

### Yawn — YN
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

Yawning is most commonly associated with boredom and tiredness. It's also a very likely sign of anxiety, and sometimes deception.

> **Color-coding rule.** The symbol in this cell is colored red, meaning that once it's paired with another 4.0-rated deceptive behavior, the yawn observed will also be scored as a 4.0.

When no deception is present and no other signs of deception are observed in the current phase of the interview, the yawn is likely nothing more than a sign of boredom or fatigue. Yawns that originate directly from boredom or tiredness tend to last almost twice as long as a yawn caused by anxiety. In some cases, a false yawn is produced prior to questioning, and although it has not been proven yet, guilty people almost exclusively exhibit this behavior (Navarro, 2011).

### Happiness — HA
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

This facial expression and its associated microexpression cause the upper parts of the face to draw upward, causing wrinkling of skin in the outer corners of the eyes and causing the eyelids to draw closer together. The Pan Am, or social smile, is mostly done using only the lower face and can appear to have unequal muscular contraction on both sides of the face.<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "Buffet, or social smile" — reconstructed as "the Pan Am, or social smile," a documented term for the polite/fake smile (contrasted with a genuine smile), which matches the described lower-face-only, asymmetric characteristics; exact original wording uncertain -->

### Flushing — FL
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Ears/Face · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

Flushing is a natural reaction in all humans, caused by an adrenaline spike. The adrenaline produced during an embarrassing moment causes vasodilation and opens up blood vessels in order to receive more oxygen (Darwin, 1872; Ekman, 1992; Crozier, 2001<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "For each 2001" — reconstructed as a researcher citation ("Crozier, 2001"), a plausible attribution given published work on the psychology of blushing; exact original name uncertain -->).

In an interview setting, the appearance of blushing may be due to embarrassment, guilt, or fear. The other gestures observed within a group will tell you which meaning the subject is communicating. Other causes of blushing or flushing to be aware of are alcohol, sexual arousal, menopause, prescription drugs, niacin, and the consumption of certain party drugs.

### Head Back — HB
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Head · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

Tilting the head back can have multiple nonverbal translations. First, many people unconsciously tilt their head backward while looking upward to retrieve information. When in conversation and trying to recall a set of data about a remembered event, subjects will often tilt their head back while attempting to retrieve information, whether it's true or false.

Tilting the head backward also exposes the vital parts of the neck. Males will typically perform this movement while challenging another person physically, while preparing for a fight. Exposing vital parts of the body to an adversary in the animal kingdom shows that one has no fear of being harmed by the other.

Variations:
- Head Back to recall data — HB1
- Head Back to expose neck — HB2

### Lip Retract — LR
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: During/After*

Lip retraction closely resembles lip compression, but the two have very different implications. When the lips go into the mouth, as mentioned in Object-Mouth (OM) earlier, there is a need for reassurance, or an uncertainty about what is being discussed or what the subject is being questioned about. This occurs equally in men and women alike.

### Ocular Orbital Tension — OT
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: Before/During*

This behavior refers to the muscular tension around the human eye. When we experience things like curiosity, focus, and intense conversational emotions, the muscles around the eye can contract and appear to make the ocular opening smaller. Squinting developed in humans in order to temporarily allow the eye to see things with slightly clearer focus, by slightly modifying the shape of the eye and reducing the amount of light taken in by the pupils.

Variations:
- Orbital Tension, classical — OT1
- Orbital Tension associated with disgust — OT2

### Eyebrow Narrowing — BN
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: Before/During*

Narrowing of the brow is typically associated with anger in Westernized culture, and is almost universally recognized as being connected to deep levels of concentration as well. This usually presents itself before and during the question or answer phase, and depending on context, indicates an angered emotional response to the current subject, or an emotionally related thought during the question phase of the interview.

Variations:
- Brow Narrowing associated with anger — BN1
- Brow Narrowing associated with contemplation — BN2

### Ventilation — VH
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Neck · Deception Rating: 3.0 · Timeframe: BDA*

As heat builds in the body — driven by the stress, anxiety, and adrenaline spikes commonly associated with deception — subjects perform various behaviors intended to lower body temperature. Sweating is common in both the guilty and the innocent, but the need to physically ventilate the body appears far more often in deceptive, high-stress subjects.

Using clothing and other objects, ventilation behavior can appear in several forms: pulling or tugging at a shirt collar to force air into the shirt and let heat escape (most commonly seen in male subjects), pulling the front of a shirt away from the body to ventilate heat, moving long hair away from the back of the neck to allow airflow, and adjustments in which a subject lifts their bottom from the chair for an unusually long period. Regardless of which specific form is exhibited, all of these behaviors are intended to pacify the same underlying need — which is why ventilation is grouped into a single category, without variable fluctuations, in the cell.

### Adam's Apple Jump — AA
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Neck · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: BA*

A sudden rise of the Adam's apple can indicate disagreement and stress. The conflicting gesture is the facial expression of fear — in this instance, the Adam's apple jump is directly associated with the reticular activating system, a precursor to the fight-or-flight response.

Variations:
- AA1 — Adam's apple jump associated with a swallow
- AA2 — Adam's apple jump without an immediate swallow

## Arms, Hands & Shoulders

### Guiding — GG
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

A guiding gesture is used when telling or showing someone where something is, or where to go. When someone points to an object or direction, or emits any form of nonverbal communication with the intent of guiding the gaze or direction of travel of another person, it's considered a guiding gesture. This is the gesture frequently made by a hostess at a restaurant, signaling a guest to follow them to the table.

Variations:
- GG1 — Guiding with the hands
- GG2 — Guiding with head motion
- GG3 — Guiding by removal of self from the path

### Baton Gestures — BG
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Arm · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

The gestures made while speaking, used to accentuate syllabic and emotional punctuation, are all baton gestures. These hand motions seem to keep time with the spoken rhythm. The important things to take away when observing baton gestures are their intensity, capillary presence, and whether or not the baton gesture is in synchronous rhythm with the spoken words. An asynchronous baton gesture can indicate a rift between what's being felt and what's being said — subjects will very often gesticulate in a way that coincides with the emotion of their statement, and when there is a timing gap, the emotion expressed is almost never genuine.

Digital Flexion (DF) is listed as a conflicting gesture here — not because it implies a different meaning, but because it implies a different emotional state. DF serves as a gauge indicating anger, stress, anxiety, or negativity associated with the baton gesture.

### Vertical Headshake — YE
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Head · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

A vertical headshake is a motion used in the United States and most other countries to indicate "yes." The conflicting gestures here are protective and barrier behaviors — these include covering the body, closing the legs, and protecting the genitals. Look for these conflicting behaviors to occur almost simultaneously with the headshake, and to be associated with LC (Lip Compression) and an increase in BR (Blink Rate).

### Head Support — HS
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

While this behavior is performed by way of an arm and hand movement, it's listed under the head due to its body-region priority. The support gesture involving the hand resting has two basic identifiable variations.

Variations:
- **Variation 1** — The chin rests on the hand, or the head is tilted to rest on a hand near the ear. The amount of surface contact here can often indicate the level of boredom or the intensity of fatigue in a subject. If the boredom is feigned, it's far more likely you'll see a more upward gaze and slightly less than the usual amount of hand-to-face contact.
- **Variation 2** — Involves an HT (Head Tilt), tilting the head to expose the side of the neck. The meanings associated with HT also apply here — the subject is likely feeling trusting or curious, or might be attempting to show defiance, if performed in the course of an interviewer's questioning or remarks.

### Surprise — SP
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 1.0*

The most authentic facial expression of surprise causes the eyebrows to raise, exposing the sclera — the white part of the eye — above the iris. The lower jaw also drops in response to genuine surprise. Even when surprise is being suppressed, a small but noticeable drop of the lower jaw can still be seen in a subject's face. It would take countless hours of training and/or practice to successfully conceal surprise on the face.

### Blink Rate — BR
*Body Region: Eyes*

The blink rate — the number of times a subject blinks per minute — varies with exposure to emotional and physical stimulus. Being a variable cell, the BR factor is given a one-to-five rating within the table.

When humans are captivated, interested, or otherwise curious about something in their field of view, the blink rate will slow and gradually decline as interest peaks. Conversely, an increasing or rapid blink rate is indicative of high stress, and is associated with low levels of concentration and interest. Watching a great movie causes blink rates to decline; taking the SAT causes a marked increase in blinking. During courtship, increases in blinking can be attributed to attraction. Rapid blinking during conversation could also be interpreted as a feeling of superiority and contempt.

> **Conflicting gesture.** CT (Chin Thrust) is listed as a conflicting gesture here, due to the deliberate decrease in BR caused by anger or a physical threat or challenge.

A blink rate is recorded with its numerical value immediately after the gesture name in the interrogator's notebook — e.g., BR3.

### Pupil Dilation — PD
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

The pupils have only one physiological function — to control the amount of light passing into the eye. However, when humans experience emotional fluctuations, the pupils can respond by signaling that emotional response to a subject, person, or photograph that's present.

In response to pleasurable, emotional, or visual stimuli, the pupils expand and dilate. Babies looking at their mothers will do this. Prospective mates will show pupil dilation. Even exposure to photos of baby animals can cause the pupils to dilate significantly. Exposure to disgusting, repulsive, ugly, or traumatic images and subjects causes the pupils to constrict — with a consistent light source, this can be rather easy to spot.

> **Notation.** Documentation of pupillary constriction is annotated as -PD (minus PD), because within all cells in the BTE, a minus-symbol prefix indicates a lack of, or the opposite of, what follows.

Variations:
- PD — Pupil dilation
- -PD — Pupil constriction

### Squint — SQ
*Gesture Type: Open/Unsure · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Squinting has so many Hollywood connotations, but it's rarely seen this way in real life. The squint is primarily a response to stress, disagreement, or anxiety. It can also signify incredulity, confusion, or deep concentration. If the squint is seen simultaneously with a head tilt and raised eyebrows, it's almost certainly incredulity. If the head is tilted while squinting and the eyebrows are narrowed or lowered, deep concentration is the more likely cause.

It's important to note the difference between orbital tension and the squint. A squint involves a more pronounced rise of the cheek muscles — the levator labii superioris and zygomaticus<!-- ASR? verify: source audio garbled as "a tall ABI superioris, and cygomaticus"; reconstructed as the facial muscles levator labii superioris and zygomaticus, which lift the cheek and match the anatomical description given -->. The cheeks will seemingly lift when the squint is performed, whereas the small degree of muscular tension involved in orbital tension rarely raises the cheeks in the same way.

### Shoulder Shrug — SH
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Neck · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The shoulder shrug has been documented since Darwin first wrote about it in his journal musings in the late 1800s. Today, the shrug is widely accepted to mean one of about four things, depending on contextual detail:

- Uncertainty, amplified by HT (Head Tilt)
- Surrender, amplified by EF (Eyebrow Flash)
- Fear, amplified by FR (Fear)
- Denial of guilt, amplified by NO (the horizontal headshake)

Some books and research articles cite this behavior as being possibly deceptive; as observed by Gofman (1963), it is mostly fear-based. The single-sided shoulder shrug, however, is much more likely to be associated with deception in an interview scenario.

> **Note.** Don't confuse SH with its single-sided cousin, SS (Single Shoulder Shrug) — the two-sided shrug maps to one of several emotional states, while the one-sided version points specifically toward deception.

### Sadness — SA
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The sadness facial expression is nothing more than a pronounced effect of gravity on the face, caused by a full relaxation of the facial and ocular muscles. The listed conflicting element here is jaw clenching (JC), due to its likelihood to immediately precede anger and violence.

### Disgust — DG
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This facial expression is seen when someone is experiencing emotional or physical disgust. If you were to imagine putting your nose into a jug full of rotting milk, this expression would show on your face — all of the parts of the face can be seen drawing toward the middle of the face.

When this facial expression is seen in social and interview settings, the subject is usually genuinely experiencing the emotion behind it. Subjects almost never feign this expression, and it is highly reliable for observation. The conflicting gesture listed here is Nostril Wing Dilation (WD) — this nostril dilation may indicate a need for air more than disgust.

### Fear — FR
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: B, D*

The fear facial expression causes the eyes to widen and exposes the sclera around the top and bottom of the iris. The lips stretch horizontally backward, and the eyebrows rise outward. Conflicting gestures listed here are Arms Behind Back (BB) and Arms in Air (IA) — these two behaviors could indicate that the facial expression is actually the result of a big, bright smile, in the case of BB, or intense excitement, in the case of IA.

### Contempt — CO
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Face · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The contempt facial expression is the only genuine facial expression that shows stronger on one side of the face. Contempt is best described as a self-assured feeling of moral or social superiority.

Note that this expression became popular in recent television shows such as *Lie to Me* and *Criminal Minds*. Though it is a recognized human expression, recent societal influences and shifts have made it a gesture now commonly seen in flirtatious and courting behavior.

### Anger — AG
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Face/Arms · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This facial expression forces the eyebrows down and presses the lips together. EF (Eyebrow Flash) is listed as a conflicting behavior — not because it negates the emotion of anger, but because its presence implies the anger stems from innocence, a result of innocent protest during an interview.

### Protecting Gestures — PR
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: B, D, A*

Protecting gestures involve one or more of the limbs crossing over the body to cover a vital area or piece of property. This is closely associated with anxiety, insecurity, and uncertainty about a person or subject. It is, however, a closed gesture, in that it allows limited access to the body.

The conflicting gesture observed alongside PR is JC (Jaw Clench) — usually a masked-anger behavior that directly conflicts with the meaning of PR.

### Swallowing — SW
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Neck · Deception Rating: 3.5 · Timeframe: B, D*

When associated with deception-detection research, swallowing behavior is inconclusive at best. We do know that excess saliva is produced during activation of the reticular activating system, and the spike in adrenaline that immediately follows it. There is no way to determine whether a swallow is due to stress, anxiety, or deception.

The reason it's given a red color code is that if we start to see several other 4.0-rated behaviors exhibited in the same behavior group, we can assume it's associated with a deceptive mindset. Exceptions to this, of course, would be excessively thirsty or dehydrated subjects, and subjects who swallow regularly in the baseline.

### Elbow Closure — EC
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

This behavior can only be observed in a seated subject. It involves the inward drawing of the elbows as the subject sits at a table. This drawing-together instinct has its origins in the same place as Protecting Gestures (PR) — it is designed to bring non-vital body parts in front of vital ones.

This is especially common in deceptive behavior during police interrogations studied in the United States. The gesture indicates discomfort, insecurity, and a need for protection. This behavior is also affected by temperature, as reflected in the color scheme.

### Single Shoulder Shrug — SS
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Shoulder · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

The single-sided shoulder shrug is a gesture almost exclusively tied to doubt and disbelief. The deception timeframe specifically states that this will be seen almost exclusively during verbal expression, as opposed to any other time.

Alone, of course, this is not enough to throw a flag — other high-rated deceptive behaviors must accompany it. Anytime a subject shrugs a single shoulder, it indicates disbelief in what the subject is saying, or in what is being discussed at the moment it occurs.

### Digital Extension — DE
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Digital Extension refers to the extension of the fingers. It reflects a relatively low level of stress and anxiety and is not associated with deception. This can occur at any time and in any position, seated or standing — the extension of the fingers and the opening of the hands signifies openness and relaxation.

In some cases, this behavior is also seen when a subject is fearful, appearing alongside Gpr (Genital Protection).

### Palm Exposure — PE
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The exposure of the palms and the exposure of the wrists both fall within this behavioral cell. This behavior is displayed any time a subject wants to appear trustworthy, non-threatening, or honest. It's frequently seen when witnessing denials and excuses from teens and adolescents, but human beings do not grow out of this behavior — it's just as common to see it in a murder investigation as it is in a missing-candy inquiry with children.

In interviews, deceptive palm exposure will present itself with less bodily and facial involvement than genuine palm exposure. The absence of raised shoulders, facial expressions, and the noticeable vocal shifts that typically accompany real emotion is more likely to indicate deception.

### Chin Stroke — CS
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This behavior, in which a subject strokes or rubs the chin, is mostly contemplative and indicates curiosity, thought, and internal processing.

> **Watch for the pairing.** When Chin Stroke occurs alongside Co (Contempt), it can indicate condescension or a deviant, superior attitude toward the subject's audience.<!-- ASR? verify: transcript's original phrase was garbled ("cont-impressive ownership or deviant thought") — rendered here as condescension/superiority toward the audience -->

### Pupil Constriction — PC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Eyes · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Pupil Constriction is a relative measurement in the interview process. Regardless of a subject's initial pupil size, any decrease in size qualifies to be registered as a PC. Pupils normally constrict to restrict the amount of light entering the eye, but they also respond to emotional stimulus — seeing or hearing something we have a serious aversion to will cause the pupils to constrict.

Some subjects will experience much more drastic contractions than others, and there is no way to measure this or predict how a given subject will respond before an interview begins. It's especially difficult to note a size change in subjects with dark-colored irises; this should be noted in the notebook if desired.

### Elbows Out — EO
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: V-1.0*

This cell refers to three types of variable behaviors related to the outward motion of the elbows. This is an opening gesture — it exposes the vital organs and is seen when a subject suddenly becomes confident or feels more secure in their environment.

Variations:
- EO1: Elbows moving outward, less than shoulder-width apart
- EO2: A single elbow moving outward during the interview
- EO3: Elbows moving outward farther than shoulder-width apart

### Posture — PS
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Pelvic · Deception Rating: V-2.0*

Posture is highly variable and, like other behaviors, should only be recorded if a shift occurs within the scope of the interview. Posture itself is readily observable, but it cannot be attributed to any single likely root meaning — it's such a baseline behavior that it often plays no part in revealing a subject's current mood.<!-- ASR? verify: source audio badly garbled here ("The pagaris is observable as such... cannot be attributed to a likely root meaning..."); rendered to preserve the stated claim that posture is observable but interpretively unreliable as a baseline behavior -->

Variations:
- PS1: A convex posture
- PS2: A concave posture

### No (Headshake) — No
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Head · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This behavior is simply the horizontal shaking of the head to indicate "no," as is done by roughly 90% of the world's population. Its relevance here, however, lies in the moments when it occurs during a statement in which the subject is affirming or attempting to confirm something — saying aloud that something is true while the headshake reveals what they're actually thinking.

### Locked Fingers — LF
*Gesture Type: Closed/Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

This behavior is not, in itself, a reliable sign of stress or anxiety without confirming microphysiological indicators and a determination of which variation is being seen in the interview. Subjects who lock their fingers are showing attempted self-restraint, and will likely also exhibit other restraining behaviors, such as shallow breathing and wrapping their legs around the legs of the chair. It's important to note the degree of digital flexion when the subject locks their fingers.

Variations:
- LF1: Fingers locked loosely and restfully together
- LF2: Fingers locked with visible signs of capillary or muscular tension

> **Scoring note.** LF2 is likely indicative of stress and anxiety, and if it's associated with other high-scoring behaviors, its rating should be moved up to 2.0.

### Steeple — ST
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

The steeple occurs when a subject is seated and the palms of the hands are facing each other while only the tips of the fingers touch. Steepling the fingers is a sign of internal confidence regarding the social setting or the particular subject matter at hand.

The height at which the steeple is held carries its own meaning. Someone holding the steeple high is far less likely to be listening and absorbing than a subject who is holding the steeple close to the surface of a table or at near waist level.

- **ST1** — Steeple at head height
- **ST2** — Steeple at chest height
- **ST3** — Steeple at waist height or lower

### Hands Behind Head — BH
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

This behavior typically exhibits a false confidence and is very closely associated with feelings of contempt and insecurity. It is a deliberate dominance gesture, intended to make the subject appear less concerned with the situation around him. Hands clasped behind the head can also indicate the presence of a feeling of ownership over a group or person with whom the subject is socially engaged.

### Arms on Hips — AH
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Shoulder · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Placing the arms on the hips is indicative of more than one meaning, given contextual variations. The thumbs going backward can indicate a desire to appear in charge in a male, and a desire to present the body to a mate in a female. Context plays the largest role in determining the meaning of this behavior.

The direction the thumbs point also shows a variation in meaning: thumbs pointing forward indicate an emotional tendency toward curiosity and interest.

- **AH1** — Arms on hips, with thumbs facing backward
- **AH2** — Arms on hips, with thumbs facing forward

### Self-Hug — SHG
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The self-hug differs from the arm cross in that it involves both arms actively embracing the subject's body, with the palms facing the body and the thumbs down, not pointing upward. This is a closed and uncertain gesture used to self-soothe, giving the subject reassurance when stress or anxiety is high. This self-hugging behavior is slightly more common in women.

> **Watch the thumbs.** If the subject's thumbs are facing upward and the palms are making contact with the body, the subject is exhibiting confidence and relaxation rather than the anxious self-soothing the base gesture normally signals.

### Arms Behind Back — BB
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Shoulder · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Placing the arms behind the back indicates one of two basic categories of associated thoughts, depending on how the hands are positioned.

- **BB1** — The subject holds the hands clasped behind the back, indicating confidence and sometimes superiority.
- **BB2** — The subject holds a wrist or part of the arm with the opposing hand. This is indicative of self-restraint in times of anger, and the higher the grip sits on the opposing arm, the higher the level of anger. While it will sometimes be displayed for confidence, it is much more likely to be a display of restraint.

### Constantly Raised Shoulders — CR
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Arm · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This can happen for a few different reasons depending on contextual influences, but it will almost always be a sign of social fear or self-consciousness. The raising of the shoulders is a vital-organ protection mechanism that occurs automatically when a subject experiences fear of any kind.

### Single Arm Wrap — WP
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Arm · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The single arm wrap is mostly performed by women. This behavior is a self-conscious signal that involves the crossing of a single arm across the midsection of the body. Women typically display this behavior in social settings where they know few people or are feeling threatened or insecure. This is common in schools with new students during the first several weeks of class — as females in groups begin to socially connect with others, the arm-wrap behavior begins to diminish accordingly.

### Freeze — FZ
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Arm · Deception Rating: 3.5 · Timeframe: B, D*

When the cognitive system of a subject becomes overloaded with scenario management and behavior regulation during deception, the movement of the body will sometimes come almost to a complete stop. The amount of mental energy exerted to manage the deception leaves little room for authentic gesture management.

> **The exception.** The fear facial expression is exempt from this pattern — the reticular activating system in the brain overrides movement in order to focus on more important things, like threats to safety.

### Facial Touching — FT
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D, A*

Perhaps no other gesture has gained so much notoriety for being deceptive as the facial touch. Touching the face, as noted in over 90 behavioral studies, has been shown to be the most common behavior displayed by study subjects during deliberate deception.

As with all elements, though, the facial touch is no more important than any other. In study settings, the gesture may only become more prevalent due to a lack of interviewer dominance, decreased stress levels causing no leakage elsewhere on the body, and the overall safety of knowing that a study is being conducted — causing participants to relax more than if they were lying about a more important, real-world matter.

### Throat Clasp — TTC
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D, A*

Clasping of the throat is almost identical to facial touching, and an operator can assume that the subject is feeling all the associated meanings of facial touching. This behavior, however, is specifically related to fear.

## Torso, Pelvis & Legs

### Arms in Air — IA
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Arms in Air is the physical comfort displayed when the arms rise — the classic image of a fan's arms shooting up during a Sunday football touchdown is a great example of this behavior. Comfortable subjects have no problem exposing their underarms and moving their arms freely, wherever they feel comfortable, with fluidity. This demonstrates comfort, elation, and excitement. Subjects who display this gesture while telling a story are demonstrating a high level of comfort with the interviewer and everyone else involved in the conversation.

### Figure-4 Leg Cross — 4
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This distinctly American body movement is associated with comfort, relaxation, and trust. The crossing of one ankle over the opposite knee is seen around the world, though sociogestural tolerance for it varies<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "frequently seen around the world as socio gestural tolerance spread"; exact original wording uncertain -->. ### Pelvic Tilt — TLT
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Pelvic · Deception Rating: 2.0 (variable)*

This variable movement is visible almost immediately when observing a subject. It's often overlooked, yet it can provide crucial information to the sharp-eyed observer. ### Facing (Pelvis-Facing Direction) — FF
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Pelvic · Deception Rating: 2.0 (variable)*

Even when the upper body isn't facing the direction of a subject's greatest interest, the pelvis often is. When standing, the pelvis will frequently follow the subject's object of mental focus, revealing where their attention is actually directed regardless of where their torso or face is pointed.

Variations:
- FF1 — facing away from the interviewer
- FF2 — facing the interviewer

### Hands in Pocket — PO
*Gesture Type: Open/Closed · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 (variable)*

Seeing a subject's hands placed into their pockets — or simply noticing a subject already in this position — can give vital clues to their internal thoughts in the moment. There are three variations in this behavior set.

Variations:
- PO1 — hands in pockets with thumbs inside the pockets, indicating a lack of confidence, uncertainty, and a willingness to show deference
- PO2 — hands in pockets with thumbs in and fingers out, indicating situational confidence and, contextually, sexual availability
- PO3 — hands in pockets with only the fingertips inserted into the opening, indicating readiness and/or reservation, contextually

### Chest Touch — TCH
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BD*

Touching of the chest is a behavior commonly seen in movies — a woman clutching her chest upon receiving bad news from a sheriff at the door is a familiar example<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "commonly seen in movies by women when they receive bad information on a sheriff"; reconstructed as the classic film trope of a death notification at the door, exact original wording uncertain -->. In real interactions, it is an unsure and nervous gesture produced in response to stressful or anxiety-producing stimuli.

### Double Cross — DC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: B–D*

The Double Cross carries a meaning quite different from a figure-4 cross or a traditional cross at the knees. It occurs when the legs are crossed in the traditional over-the-knee manner, and then the foot crosses once more around the lower leg. This is a very distinct gesture that indicates withholding and probable concealment.

### Tapping — TP
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: DA*

Tapping fingers on a table can have multiple meanings. It's contextually dependent and can indicate boredom, inner thoughts, nervousness, or tension release — there is no single tapping pattern that reliably points to one meaning over another. Context must always be taken into account before making a judgment call on a scenario or scoring an interrogation.

### Fidgeting — FI
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BD*

Fidgeting can be performed by a subject with or without an object in hand. It typically involves seemingly non-productive movement not intended to accomplish any specific task, and it can involve any part of the body — the movement functions, whether consciously or not, to help control feelings of anxiety and stress<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "Further a body part is away from the head, But whether it is to control during times of anxiety and stress"; reconstructed as fidgeting occurring in any body part and serving to help manage anxiety and stress, exact original wording uncertain -->. The accumulation of nervous energy and the buildup of adrenaline causes the body to need to burn off the excess by making small muscular movements in the extremities.

### Two-Handed Grasping — GRS
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BD*

This is the behavior wherein a subject grasps an object — a bag, an article of clothing, or any other item<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "Yeah, sorry, leg, and get clothing or any other output"; reconstructed as a list of grasped objects, exact original wording uncertain --> — with both hands. This is a closed gesture, but it can also be displayed during anxiety and could indicate a need for reassurance and control. Women who are untrusting or overly conscious of their environment will often do this to their purses. ### Back of Neck — BON
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BA*

Hands will draw rearward to the back of the neck in situations that present the subject with stress, anxiety, or deep contemplative thinking.

### Lint Picking — LP
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

Lint Picking is a self-conscious behavior that manifests as picking lint — real or imaginary — off one's own clothing or a nearby object. This behavior signals nervous energy and insecurity. A subject who is uncomfortable in conversation will pick at their clothing in order to occupy themselves productively during the passing time, giving them an excuse for not talking.

### Wrist-Forehead — WF
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: Before/After*

Wrist-Forehead is similar in all regards to the Ventilation gesture, in that it communicates a pacifying of stress or a need to reduce internal conflict or anxiety. This behavior, in theory, calms the subject down, and is used when the need for self-assurance arises.

### Barrier Gesture — BAR
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 3.0 · Timeframe: Before*

The Barrier Gesture is somewhat difficult to pin down in a social context. Arms will typically move in various directions throughout a conversation, but the Barrier Gesture is specifically a behavior in which an arm comes into a direct line between the interviewer and the subject. This is a subtle and mostly unconscious gesture that occurs in sometimes fleeting but significant increments. In a social context, this can be a way for a person to feel more secure and protected in an environment that causes them to feel slightly threatened or insecure.

### Barrier Cross — BC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 3.0 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

Barrier Cross differs from the Figure-4 and the traditional crossing of the legs. Here, the legs are pointed mostly toward the interviewer and are crossed at the calf, with one leg placed over the knee. This is a closed gesture, and where the other leg-crossing gestures indicate comfort and relaxation, the Barrier Cross is indicative of withholding and concealment. It can also reflect nervous tension associated with being interviewed, or with the appearance of the interviewer.

### Palms Down — PDN
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 3.5 · Timeframe: During/After*

Palms Down occurs for various reasons and indicates a lack of willingness to be transparent, honest, and forthcoming in an interview — essentially, a lack of the subject's willingness to "show their hands," so to speak. During interviews, this is typically seen when a subject becomes threatened as an operator begins to expose a flaw in their statements. In social scenarios, this may be as harmless as a subject disagreeing with the operator without wishing to voice their opinion. Subjects who are withholding information will also perform this while listening to questions, before they even speak.

### Groin Protecting — GPR
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

Groin Protecting is an unconscious behavior that indicates a subject's immediate need to protect themselves when a verbal, physical, or psychological threat is presented to them. Imagine a man who isn't accustomed to public speaking, standing on a stage while being introduced to a large crowd — the need to cover the genitals is innate in most men. This gesture is seen when a subject is feeling vulnerable, or otherwise insecure.

### Hushing — HU
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

Hushing — a term coined by Barbara and Allan Pease, the body-language researchers and authors of *The Definitive Book of Body Language* — is seen when the subject brings their hands to their face and performs any behavior that covers the mouth. This is considered a hushing gesture for the BTE. Hushing is seen when a subject becomes nervous or self-conscious about what they are saying or thinking.

Three normal variations exist for this behavior.

Variations:
- HU1: Hushing via covering the mouth, or a cough
- HU2: Hushing via covering of the lips with the fingers
- HU3: Hushing via scratching of the nose

### Groin Exposure — GE
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

Groin Exposure occurs when the subject opens the legs or angles them in the direction of the interviewer. Many times, this shows postural vulnerability, honesty, and a willingness to communicate the truth.

### Leg Crossing — LG
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

Leg Crossing is seen when the legs cross in the traditional manner, where one knee is crossed over the other and the legs remain mostly together. This is indicative of trust and comfort.

> **Watch the reversal.** While this gesture is important to note during an interview, its opposite is of even greater value: the uncrossing of the legs indicates the opposite of the meaning just stated.

### Toes Up — AGG
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Feet · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: Deception Not Likely*

Toes Up is an anti-gravity gesture frequently seen together with the Arms-in-Air gesture, and is usually a relief, celebratory, or exclamatory gesture. This indicates elatedness and happiness. It occurs when the toes of a subject's shoes are lifted slightly off the ground. The common rise of the foot is one inch or less.

### Breathing Rate — BRE
*Body Region: Torso*

The subject's breathing rate is a fluctuating factor throughout the interview. The average breathing rate for a healthy adult is 12 to 20 breaths per minute. Breathing rate will typically increase in proportion to the amount of stress the subject is experiencing.

### Facing (Torso-Facing Direction) — FC
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Torso · Deception Rating: 2.0 (variable)*

Facing describes the direct frontal or away-facing orientation of the torso — related to, but distinct from, the twisting of the torso. For instance, the subject's torso may be pointed slightly away from the interviewer at the beginning of a question group, and the question itself causes the subject to immediately turn his full body to face the interviewer while answering or listening. Conversely, the complete opposite can also occur, where the torso turns completely away from the interviewer.

Variations:
- FCT — Facing torso toward the interviewer
- FCA — Facing torso away from the interviewer

### Postural Tilt — PT
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Torso · Deception Rating: 2.0 (variable)*

Within any social encounter, especially one involving only two parties, the tilt of the torsos toward and away from one another is a constant and revealing behavior from both people about their thoughts. Leaning toward another person shows interest in that person. When two people lean in toward each other, they are communicating mutual interest. When one leans in and the other leans back, the person leaning back is showing a lack of interest, comfort, higher status, or trust.

Variations:
- Posture leaning toward
- Posture leaning away

### Knee Hugging — KH
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Arms · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: Before*

This is a rare occurrence in an interview: the subject, while seated, wraps both arms around their legs, pulled up toward the chest. The knees have been hypothesized to stand in for an imaginary person — subjects will hug their own knees when no real person is available to pacify anxiety or stress through hugging.

### Digital Flexion — DF
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: Before/During/After*

Flexion — the drawing-in of the fingers toward the palm — is a fantastic in-the-moment barometer of the subject's anxiety at that specific moment. Noticing even a small movement of the fingers during an interview can provide a great deal of information when paired with the associated verbal and emotional content within the interview question group. The further the fingers draw into the palm, the more momentary anxiety the subject is feeling.

### Binding — BI
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: During/After*

This occurs when the legs come together and show no relaxation. The behavior also frequently brings the feet together to touch. Binding is associated with secretiveness, fear, anxiety, worry, and mistrust.

### Locked Ankle — LA
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Hands/Feet · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: Before/After*

This occurs when the ankles are locked, usually under a chair. Subjects who are unwilling to provide much information, or who are withholding or concealing information, will do this. It's best to deliberately break this behavior by asking the subject to stand or move in a way that causes their ankles to unlock.

### Grooming — GM
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Legs · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: After*

Any behavior conducted with the intention of improving physical appearance or visual presentation is a grooming behavior. These behaviors exhibit themselves in various forms and are relevant to a degree when connected with the stimulus that causes them. Variations in upbringing can increase or decrease the frequency of these gestures — a subject<!-- ASR? verify: source audio reads "So James who experienced an upbringing" — reconstructed as "a subject," the most plausible reading given no individual named "James" appears elsewhere in this chapter; exact original wording uncertain --> who experienced an upbringing that placed more value on a neat appearance will revert to grooming behaviors during times of anxiety. Noting this in your notebook, or your mental notebook, should be an easy observation to make in conversation, regardless of the environment.

### Thigh Clasp — THC
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: Before*

This is more prevalent in women, though men can also display it. This auto-contact behavior is commonly displayed during physical attraction and sexual arousal. Women will do this in a way similar to a pacifying behavior, in that the hands are taking the place of another person — but instead of soothing them, they are caressing them. In interviews, subjects will perform this behavior to control themselves and to minimize the amount of stress they feel.

> **Not quite pacifying.** The thigh clasp isn't a pacifying behavior, though it serves a similar purpose. Imagine it as another person beside the subject, inflicting slight discomfort to prevent the release of information or deceptive behavior.

### Wrist Touch — WT
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: During*

This is another form of auto-contact behavior, involving the touching of one's own wrists. It's listed as a closed gesture due to its barrier-creating properties and its meaning in social context. Wrist touch functions as both a barrier and a comforting movement, giving subjects a sense of attention and security when experiencing social pressure, nervousness, or anxiety.

This is often seen in men, in public, in the form of reaching across and using one hand to adjust a wristwatch or shirt cuff on the other hand. Performing this same reaching-across motion to pop knuckles, rub hands, adjust a sleeve, or any other similar action is all considered WT.

The second variation is when a subject reaches across and physically grasps the wrist of the opposite hand. This is an anger-related, self-restraint behavior that sends a completely different signal — hence the red lettering.

Variations:
- WT1 — Dusting
- WT2 — Grabbing

> **Red lettering.** WT2 (grasping the wrist) signals anger and self-restraint rather than the comfort-seeking meaning of WT1 — a distinct, more aggressive read on the table.

### Inward Toe Pointing — IP
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Feet · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: B, A*

Inward toe pointing happens very slightly more often in women than in men, and it indicates self-consciousness. Compared with barrier behaviors, it can indicate concealment, mistrust, or withholding. The toes point inward when people feel insecure, or when a subject wishes to occupy less space. This behavior also offers some protection to the genitals, and it can be read as a response to threatening scenarios or uncomfortable social environments.

### Eyelid Rub — ER
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: B, D, A*

Eyelid rubbing is another hand-to-face gesture, and it carries signs of concern in addition to the stress that's already signaled by any hand-to-face contact. Rubbing the eyelid is performed mostly by men and can indicate the need to stop thinking of something, or to end a train of thought.

### Knee Clasp — KC
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: A*

The knee clasp is indicative of a need to leave or end the conversation. This takes place only in the seated position, when a subject — usually with both hands — takes hold of their knees. It's usually followed by a longer inhale than normal.

### Foot Withdraw — FW
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Feet · Deception Rating: 3.5 · Timeframe: B, A*

Foot withdrawal is the sudden withdrawal of the feet during conversation while seated, drawing rearward underneath the chair. This behavior indicates a sudden and immediate need to withdraw or self-protect, and it can also indicate a loss of trust in the interviewer, self-consciousness, and concealment.

### Finger-Nose — FNS
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Hands · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: B, D*

Finger-Nose is a variation of a hushing gesture, with the added meaning of concealing the instinct to cover the mouth. This is a high-rated behavior in that it's one of the most deceptive in all of non-verbal deception research.

> **Read it in company.** This behavior alone might not score high enough on the scale to be qualified as deceptive on its own; when it's seen in interviews, it's almost always accompanied by a similarly rated gesture.

## Object Interaction

### Interaction with Other's Property — OPI
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This is the subject's willingness to physically interact with property belonging to people who aren't familiar to them, and it can indicate their comfort level and, sometimes, their contempt for those around them. Typically, subjects will handle another person's property in the same manner they feel toward that person; if they're careless with the property, the assumption can safely be made that they aren't fond of them. In BTE observations, we simply note the presence or absence of a willingness to interact with such property.

Variations:
- **OPI+** — voluntary interaction with another's belongings
- **OPI−** — unwillingness to interact, even when presented the opportunity to do so

### Object Interaction — OI
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Where subjects interact with objects in the room and the furniture around them, this can be a highly accurate indicator of their comfort and confidence levels. A subject's willingness to adjust objects <!-- ASR? verify: "put job gents" -- best guess: adjust/touch objects --> and move chairs to suit their comfort level shows how willing they are to take charge of their environment, and it can be revealing.

A subject wishing to put on the best deception of their life will likely do everything possible not to disturb the environment. This in itself isn't necessarily a deceptive indicator, because guilty and innocent subjects alike will always show different baselines of confidence and comfort.

### Shoe Removal — SR
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

The willingness to display removal of a shoe, or unconscious fidgeting with footwear, shows signs of comfort, confidence, and trust. This can occur in numerous ways, and, if seen, it should be interpreted as a sign of subject comfort and trust.

### Carelessness — BC
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

This behavior refers to the subject's carelessness with his or her own property. Whether conscious or unconscious, a display of carelessness communicates a careless attitude and an aloof nature that isn't conducive to a good interview. Examples of this include throwing phones, keys, and objects onto the table, dropping heavily into a seat, yanking a chair around to sit, and throwing a jacket onto the back of a chair.

### Watch Checking — WC
*Gesture Type: Open · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.0 · Timeframe: DNL*

Watch checking is a commonly understood behavior and is easily spotted, even by novice interviewers. Pay particular attention to whether the checking of the watch involves the subject using two hands, and whether the opposing hand places an unusual amount of tension while holding the watch to view it.

### Jacket Buttoning — JB
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

This is more common in males, simply due to the type of clothing worn regularly by men. Buttoning of a jacket usually indicates a stopping point in trust-building, and can reflect a holding pattern for anxiety. This behavior is heavily affected by temperature.

### Clothing Covering — CC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: DNL*

Clothing Covering refers to the pulling or adjusting of clothing in a way that increases the amount of coverage on the body. This can be seen in women pulling a shirt down to cover themselves, lifting a collar to decrease breast exposure<!-- ASR? verify: transcript said "lifting a sleeve to decrease breast exposure" -->, and pulling sleeves down to cover more of the wrist area. This behavior is affected by temperature.

### Object Barrier — OB
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 1.5 · Timeframe: B*

Object Barrier involves the covering of the body with any object. Girls walking to school holding books across their chest, holding a drink at a bar in front of the body, using a book or clipboard as a barrier on a table, and placing objects — even cups — between the subject and the interviewer are all examples of this behavior. It indicates signs of insecurity, a need for protection, and sometimes a subject with withdrawn social tendencies.

### Chair Arms — CA
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BDA*

Chair Arms involves the wrapping of the hands around the arms of a chair. This is a sign of self-restraint and can indicate the withholding of information. Make note of the pressure with which the subject grasps the arms of the chair, and whether one hand tightens, showing more capillary response than the other. Discerning the meaning of this behavior without associated behaviors is almost impossible.

### Groin Shield — GS
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BDA*

Groin Shield is a barrier gesture that differs from other barrier behaviors in that the same movements are made specifically to cover the genital area. This carries the same meaning as groin shielding elsewhere on the table, in that this behavior is unconscious and indicates the subject's immediate need to protect themselves when a verbal, physical, or psychological threat is presented to them.

### Security Check — BS
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 2.0 · Timeframe: BDA*

Security Check occurs when a subject visually or physically interacts with an object belonging to them for the purpose of checking its safety, presence, or exact location. This is a strong indicator of distrust and shows signs of nervousness and insecurity. It's commonly seen in interviews when a female subject consciously grabs her purse and pulls it closer to her without looking. Another common example is men reaching back to check for the presence of their wallets, pulling a phone on the table closer to them, and patting a shirt pocket to ensure something is still there.

### Fists-Table — FTB
*Gesture Type: Aggressive · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 2.5 · Timeframe: DA*

Fists-Table is a highly aggressive behavior, and it's almost equally shown in deceptive and genuine subjects. Determining the difference between deceptive and honest subjects exhibiting this behavior must be done in real time — watching for associated behaviors and ensuring the timing coincides with the behavior itself.

> **Timing is the tell.** Honest behaviors happen simultaneously, whereas deceptive behaviors tend to happen microseconds apart, because they have to originate from different parts of the brain instead of one.

### Object Concealment — OC
*Gesture Type: Closed · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 3.0 · Timeframe: BDA*

Object Concealment is seen when a subject conceals something from view, or attempts to, such as when you approach a subject and they tilt a phone screen away, close the opening of a purse, or consciously draw a hand over a phone screen. This is also seen when a subject holds a phone at the side of the body, with the angle of the phone unconsciously adjusted away from the interviewer. This behavior can also indicate a desire to conceal information not contained in a subject's phone at all — the phone is simply the unconscious surrogate that's used.

### Jewelry Play — JP
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 3.5 · Timeframe: BA*

The jewelry most people wear is closely associated with identity and self-image. Playing or fiddling with jewelry can be a way for subjects to self-soothe during social stress or anxiety. It's frequently seen in women, playing with or reaching up to grasp a necklace or bracelet.

### Chair Legs — CL
*Gesture Type: Unsure · Body Region: Object · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: BDA*

When a subject wraps their ankles around the legs of a chair, as if to stay in place if someone were pulling them upward, it's marked as Cl. This behavior is a self-restraint gesture and almost always indicates the withholding of information and an unwillingness to openly participate in the conversation. This is an under-researched behavior; it has been seen more commonly in young adults during interview scenarios.

## Direct Verbal Behavior

### Vocal Hesitancy — HES
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Hesitancy seen before an answer begins is a time gap that allows the subject to process their story in their mind and ensure its soundness before vocalizing it out loud. We know this occurs because the subject displays a distinct pause prior to answering a question or making a statement.

### Psychological Distancing — PSD
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Psychological Distancing occurs when a subject euphemizes the crime or offense in question, replacing it with a less severe word. Examples include using *hurt* instead of *kill*, *take* instead of *steal*, *relations* instead of *sex*, and *touch* instead of *molest*. This is a very common deception factor employed by the subconscious to lessen the guilt experienced in the presence of interviewers — a psychological way of reducing the amount of nonverbal stress shown in the body during interviews.

### Rising Vocal Pitch — RIP
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Rising Vocal Pitch results when the stress of deceiving an interviewer, compounded by the anxiety of high-stakes failure, causes the muscles in the vocal production area to tighten and raise the natural pitch of the voice during testimony and conversation.

> **Baseline element.** Rising Vocal Pitch is measured only as a deviation from the subject's individual vocal baseline — it must not be recorded as a flat 4.0-coded behavior.

### Increase in Vocal Speed — SPD
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Increase in Vocal Speed requires the interviewer or analyst to first establish a vocal baseline, noting the cadence with which a subject normally speaks. In an effort to get the exchange over with quickly, subjects will frequently speed up their rate of speech, lessening the psychological stress they experience and exuding fewer nonverbal signs of deception during the deceptive statement.

### Non-Answer Statements — NA
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Non-Answer Statements occur when a subject replies to a question with a lengthy response that fails to actually answer the question asked. Any reply that sidesteps the question in this way qualifies as a non-answer. For example, the interviewer asks, "Did you go to the liquor store last night?" and the subject responds, "I don't even drink."

### Pronoun Absent — PRN
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Pronoun Absent statements occur because deceptive narratives sometimes lack pronouns, a byproduct of the cognitive load placed on the subject during deception. For example, when asked to recount the events of the evening, a subject might say: "Well, I woke up around two and went to the ATM at the gas station, left the bar around 2 a.m., and got back to the house around 2:30." Notice how the pronoun "I" appears once and then disappears from the rest of the account. Once you start listening closely, you'll notice the absence of pronouns cropping up more regularly in everyday conversations you have with others as well, not just in interviews.

### Resume Statements — RES
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Resume Statements are a verbal expression commonly used as an answer to an allegation or a question involving suspicion of wrongdoing. Subjects express themselves to the interviewer by giving all the reasons they aren't capable of the act in question — the volunteer work they do, where they went to school, or their position within the community. After being questioned about a theft, for example, a subject might reply: "Well, I'm a well-respected member of this community and a volunteer at the church. My wife and I both give our time to help the homeless, and our neighbors will tell you the same thing." This statement never answers the question — the subject is instead attempting to manage the way the interviewer perceives them by listing good qualities and civic involvement.

### Non-Contracting Statement — NC
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Non-Contracting Statements occur when a subject denies or rejects an accusation. In order to make the statement easier to believe, they may drop the contraction of two words — saying, for instance, "did not" instead of "didn't," or "could not" instead of "couldn't." This gives the subject the subconscious sense that they are communicating more clearly and emphatically to the interviewer. It happens below conscious awareness: the subject's mind is doing its best to remove ambiguous communication in order to make the statement sound more matter-of-fact.

### Question Reversal — QR
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Question Reversal is an aggressive reversal of questioning by the subject. Asked a question, the subject responds with a question of their own — one that implies guilt on the interviewer's part, or that is designed to challenge the interviewer's motives for asking in the first place.

### Ambiguity — AM
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Ambiguity occurs when a subject answers a question but provides vague statements about the incident, or about their account of the events in question.

### Politeness — POL
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Politeness involves a shift in the politeness level within the conversation. A subject may frequently answer questions with "yeah, yeah" and "mm-hmm," then suddenly follow another question with "yes, sir" or "no, sir, I didn't." This marks a shift in the level of politeness away from the subject's typical baseline behavior.

> **Baseline caveat.** The mere presence of politeness alone does not warrant a POL rating in analysis — it is the *shift* from the subject's own baseline that counts.

### Over-Apology — OA
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: DA*

Over-Apology occurs when the subject continually apologizes for things that happen during the interview — not being able to provide more information, giving inadequate detail, or not having what they think the interviewer is looking for. This functions as a subconscious apology for other things that have yet to be uncovered.

> **Red flag.** A sudden presence of apology and overcompensation is very much a red flag, regardless of the reason given for it.

### Mini-Confession — MC
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Mini-Confession takes the form of a confession that isn't actually about the event for which the subject is being questioned. In the subject's mind, if they confess to a few small things, they will appear more open and honest. The hope is that the interviewer will eventually either forget to ask about the bigger questions, or move past them. Mini-confessions often take place during an interview and can look like genuine, honest admissions of small, insignificant wrongdoings — but their real function is to shift the interviewer's attention away from what actually matters.

> **Watch for the misdirection.** A subject volunteering small, seemingly candid admissions may be using apparent honesty about minor matters to build credibility — and cover — for withholding something larger.

### Exclusions — EXC
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Exclusions occur when a subject's words don't fully answer the question asked — the subject deliberately excludes an item, a detail, or a piece of information they'd rather not disclose. It's a polite, low-friction way of withholding information from an interviewer without appearing evasive.

Common exclusionary phrases include:
- "As far as I know"
- "To the best of my knowledge"
- "Basically"
- "Probably"
- "I suppose"

### Chronology — CHR
*Body Region: Verbal · Deception Rating: 4.0 · Timeframe: D*

Chronology can be a difficult behavior to spot, because the interviewer has the potential to inadvertently trigger it — asking a subject to walk through events in order renders the indicator void and unusable as a scoring data point. When a subject recounts a story or chain of events in perfect chronological order unprompted, that is a Chr indicator.

Truthful, unrehearsed accounts will often come out jumbled rather than in strict sequence — the subject will almost always start with the most dramatic part of the story<!-- ASR? verify: "formatic" likely meant "dramatic" -->. However, if the interviewer directly asks the subject to recount events in the order they occurred, the chronology of their statement becomes essentially irrelevant as a deception indicator, since the interviewer prompted the ordering rather than the subject volunteering it.

> **Prompted order doesn't count.** Chronology is only meaningful as an indicator when the subject volunteers a perfectly ordered account unprompted. If the interviewer asked for events "in order," the resulting sequence tells you nothing.

## Key Takeaways

- **No single cell convicts anyone.** Almost every cell in this chapter repeats the same warning in different words: a behavior only means something in combination with its Confirming, Amplifying, and Conflicting Gestures — and in the context of the stimulus that caused it. Read cells in groups, not in isolation.
- **The four Arm Cross variations (ACC1–ACC4) carry very different weight.** Palms touching the body signals reassurance; hands wrapped around the arms signals a tighter grip on that same need; thumbs pointing upward flips the read toward confidence despite the crossed arms; and clenched fists — especially amplified by digital flexion — is the variation to watch closely.
- **A handful of cells are explicit danger signals.** Fingers wrapped around the arms and pulled toward the sternum, paired with Anger (AG), is a warning sign — and paired with Wing Dilation (WD) as well, the table treats it as a sign the subject is about to become violent.
- **Red-lettered cells are contagious.** Swallowing, Yawning, and several others carry red-letter coding: seen alone they may mean little, but if they appear alongside any other 4.0-rated behavior in the same window, they become a 4.0 themselves.
- **Blue-lettered cells are temperature-sensitive.** Ventilation, Elbow Closure, and other blue-lettered behaviors increase in frequency simply because a room is warm or cold — discount them accordingly rather than reading them as pure stress signals.
- **The highest-stress cells (4.0) cluster in a few predictable places:** Confirmation Glance, Adam's Apple Jump, Elbow Closure, Single Shoulder Shrug, Facial Touching, Throat Clasp, Finger-Nose, Fists on Table, Chair Legs — and, notably, every single cell in the Direct Verbal region. Verbal deception indicators score at the ceiling almost across the board.
- **Facial expressions are the hardest channel to fake.** Genuine surprise drops the jaw even when a subject is actively suppressing it; Disgust is almost never feigned; Contempt is the only expression that reliably shows stronger on one side of the face than the other.
- **Pupils and blink rate run in opposite directions under interest.** Genuine engagement dilates the pupils and slows the blink rate; high stress does the opposite — a rapid blink rate paired with pupil constriction points toward stress rather than attraction or absorption.
- **The same symbol can mean two unrelated things in different regions of the table.** Bc denotes both Barrier Cross (a leg gesture) and Carelessness (an object-interaction gesture) — always read a symbol in the context of the section it appears in.
- **Context can neutralize an otherwise damning cell.** A Chronology statement (Chr) scores 4.0 when volunteered — but scores nothing at all if the interviewer explicitly asked for events in order. The stimulus that produced a behavior always has the final say over what it means.
